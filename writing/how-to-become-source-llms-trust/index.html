How to Become a Source the LLMs Trust

Build a public record that people and AI-mediated systems can identify, inspect, and revisit—without turning “trust” into an empty visibility promise.

Form: evidence-led guide
Scope: public web sources
Method: inspectable record
Status: complete
Claim boundary. No page can make an LLM, search engine, or retrieval system trust, cite, or represent a source in a particular way. This guide describes practices that make a source easier to understand and verify. Selection, crawling, indexing, retrieval, citation, and traffic remain system- and context-dependent.
01 / Start with the record

“Trust” is a human shorthand. Start with a source that can be checked.

The practical goal is not to convince a model. It is to leave behind a source record a person can attribute, inspect, and maintain: who made the claim, what supports it, where it lives, when it was reviewed, and what it does not establish.

In Google Search, the term matters because Google describes helpful, reliable, people-first material as material that provides original information or analysis, shows clear sourcing and expertise, and makes authorship understandable.[1] That guidance is about Search, not a universal account of every LLM. It is still a useful design constraint for public knowledge: a claim should have a visible owner and a path back to evidence.

For AI features in Google Search, Google says the usual SEO foundations remain relevant and there are no special requirements to appear. A page must still be eligible for Search, and even a technically eligible page is not guaranteed to be crawled, indexed, or served.[2] That boundary should shape the work. Build an inspectable source because it is useful, not because a platform outcome can be promised.

02 / The source record

Four things a source should make visible.

01 / Identity

State who is speaking.

Use a stable name, a recognizable author or organization page, and a clear account of the subject the source can responsibly address. Consistency is useful when a reader needs to distinguish an author, a brand, and a related operating entity.

02 / Evidence

Show what supports the claim.

Link the primary source when one exists. Label a first-hand observation, a calculation, an interpretation, and an opinion differently. Do not substitute a citation list for an evidence trail.

03 / Access

Make the answer reachable.

Put the useful answer in readable main content, give it a descriptive title and headings, keep the URL stable, and connect it to the surrounding topic through internal links.

04 / Maintenance

Leave a revision path.

Show an update cue, preserve sources, name the unresolved questions, and correct a claim when the evidence changes. A source record is a practice, not a one-time asset.

03 / Evidence before posture

Original work gives the record something to carry.

Google’s people-first guidance asks whether content provides original information, reporting, research, or analysis; whether it adds value beyond copied or rewritten material; and whether an author’s relevant background is visible.[1] Those are productive questions for any research page. They redirect effort from surface-level “authority” language to the actual work: observations, source selection, method, and revision.

For Mason Nguyen’s research, this means making the signal legible. A framework page should distinguish its own operating language from a published standard. A visibility observation should include the platform, query, date, result capture, and limits. A systems claim should make its dependencies and decision owner visible. The point is not to sound certain; it is to make certainty proportionate to the record.

Google’s generative-search guidance similarly distinguishes non-commodity material with a unique point of view or first-hand experience from material that mainly restates available information. It cautions against creating pages for every query variation or pursuing inauthentic mentions as a visibility tactic.[3]

04 / Technical legibility

Make a good source available without inventing a secret parser.

Technical clarity is a prerequisite for access, not proof of credibility. For Google’s AI features, the stated basics include allowing crawling, making content findable through internal links, keeping important information in text, providing a usable page experience, and ensuring structured data matches visible content.[2]

Structured data has a bounded role. Google says it can provide explicit clues about a page’s meaning and may support rich-result eligibility, but the markup should describe the page it appears on and should not be used for invisible or empty content.[4] Use fewer complete, accurate properties rather than a large graph of claims a visitor cannot verify.

This site uses Article, FAQPage, BreadcrumbList, and related schema where they reflect visible content. That helps keep the public record coherent; it does not turn structured data into a citation switch. For the broader operating model, see the ARM Framework and the Entity Authority definition.

05 / Working sequence

Improve one consequential page before creating ten more.

1

Name the real question.

Choose a question a specific reader needs answered. Define the source’s purpose, audience, and scope before selecting keywords or tactics.

2

Write the answer in the main body.

Give the reader the substantive definition, method, or analysis in accessible text. Use headings to show how the answer is organized, not to simulate depth.

3

Build the evidence trail.

Link primary guidance, original documents, or stated observations. Mark interpretation and uncertainty where they begin. Date the record when freshness matters.

4

Connect the research map.

Use descriptive internal links to the definitions, methods, and adjacent questions that give the page meaning. On this site, that includes What is GEO?, AI Visibility Strategy, and the GEO Glossary.

5

Review what changed.

Maintain the sources, correct errors, and keep an observation log separate from causal claims. Search Console can reveal crawl and search data; it cannot prove that a single appearance caused a business outcome.

06 / Review table

Separate a useful signal from an unsupported inference.

What to examineA checkable signalWhat it does not prove
AuthorshipA named author, a relevant background page, and a page purpose that a reader can understand.That every model will treat the author as authoritative.
EvidencePrimary links, dates, stated method, and a distinction between observation and interpretation.That the conclusion is complete or universally accepted.
Technical structureIndexable canonicals, readable content, valid markup, stable routes, and descriptive internal links.That a page will be crawled, retrieved, linked, or cited.

07 / What to avoid

Do not manufacture the appearance of a source.

A page does not become more useful because it says “trusted by AI” more often. Avoid anonymous claims of authority, summaries with no added work, blank pages built to hold markup, fake freshness cues, and content created mainly to intercept every query variation. These choices make the record harder—not easier—to inspect.

Google explicitly says that it does not require new AI text files, special markup, or `llms.txt` for its generative features.[3] This site maintains a scope note on llms.txt because supplemental inventories can still be useful for people or systems that choose to consume them. The useful distinction is between a maintained reference and a fabricated ranking mechanism.

08 / FAQ

Questions that keep the practice honest.

Can a site make an LLM trust or cite it?

No. Different systems use different models, indexes, retrieval paths, policies, and contexts. The controllable work is to publish a clear, attributable, technically accessible, maintained source record.

Does Google require special AI schema or an llms.txt file?

No. Google says its AI features have no additional requirements or special optimizations, and that Google does not use llms.txt for generative Search. Accurate structured data still has a useful ordinary Search role when it describes visible content.[2] [4]

What is the first source-quality improvement to make?

Choose one important page and make the author, purpose, direct answer, supporting evidence, date, claim boundary, and related research path easy to find. Then review the source record after it has been used or challenged.
