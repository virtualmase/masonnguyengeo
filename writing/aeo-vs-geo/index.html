AEO vs. GEO

Useful shorthand for different discovery questions—not reliable outcome levers.

Primary query: AEO vs GEO
Type: Research comparison
Status: Published research note

The short answer

AEO and GEO are labels people use to discuss how information may be surfaced in answer-oriented and generative search experiences. They are not settled technical standards, and neither label changes the underlying requirement: publish useful, crawlable, sourceable content for people first.

For Google’s generative search features, the official guidance is direct: established Search quality, ranking, and technical practices still apply. Google cautions against special-markup theories, keyword-variation factories, and other tactics designed to manipulate generative responses rather than help readers.

What each term is trying to describe

QuestionAEOGEO

What not to inferA concise answer does not ensure a featured answer, citation, ranking, or conversion.A benchmark result does not ensure visibility, citation, traffic, ranking, or commercial performance.

The useful distinction is practical: AEO asks whether an answer can be understood and verified. GEO asks how a generative discovery environment may retrieve and synthesize source material. In practice, the two often overlap—and both collapse if the page is vague, inaccessible, or unsupported.

What Google actually says

Google’s guidance for generative AI features says its systems remain rooted in core Search ranking and quality systems. The work is familiar: create non-commodity, people-first material; make it crawlable; provide clear technical structure; and measure through Search Console.

Google also rejects a large share of the folklore around this topic. It says special AI files, forced content chunking, artificial mentions, and rewriting solely for AI systems are not requirements for its generative features. That is why this site treats machine-readable detail as a clarity tool, not an outcome promise.

What the GEO research actually shows

The KDD 2024 paper on Generative Engine Optimization formalizes generative engines as systems that synthesize information from multiple sources. Its authors introduce a black-box optimization framework and benchmark. Their reported results vary by domain.

That evidence matters because it gives the term GEO an academic reference point. It does not turn an experimental result into a universal operating claim. A responsible page should never convert a benchmark’s “up to” result into a promise about a specific site, model, audience, or commercial result.

A decision framework for the work

  1. Start with the user’s question. Make the page worth reading even if no AI surface ever references it.
  2. State the entity and the boundary. Name who is making the claim, what the page covers, and what it does not establish.
  3. Use primary sources where a claim depends on a platform or paper. Link to the original guidance or research, not a chain of summaries.
  4. Make the route technically legible. Use a stable canonical URL, headings, internal links, and accurate metadata.
  5. Measure observations, not mythology. If a discovery surface changes, record the prompt, date, source, and limitations. Do not turn one observation into a causal result.

This is the discipline behind GEO research and the public-source approach in How to Become a Source the LLMs Trust.

The cleaner conclusion

AEO and GEO can be useful language if they make a team ask better questions about answers, sources, retrieval, and verification. They become harmful when they are used as a substitute for evidence or as a promise that a model will cite, rank, or recommend a page.

Build the source before you optimize the surface. That is slower than chasing a new acronym. It is also easier to defend when the work is inspected.
